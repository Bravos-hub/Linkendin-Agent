The constraint that defines charging infrastructure in emerging markets is not the charger itself — it is the business model wrapped around it. Kenya's electric bus segment is proving this. While private EV adoption remains slow, operators like BasiGo have decoupled the vehicle from the energy supply through pay-as-you-drive programs: the bus is acquired at a reduced upfront cost, and energy is billed as a service per kilometre driven. The charger stops being a capital asset the operator must finance and becomes part of a managed energy contract.

Architecturally, this changes the engineering brief. A depot serving a financed fleet must be designed for predictable, scheduled duty cycles rather than opportunistic public charging — overnight AC or low-power DC charging in the 50 kW to 150 kW class, staggered to stay within the site's grid connection capacity, with OCPP-managed load balancing across stalls. Real-time monitoring and analytics, bundled into the same service contract, close the loop between energy delivered, kilometres driven, and revenue collected.

The partnership layer is the real story. Charging providers, bus OEMs, financiers, and fleet operators are forming vertically integrated stacks rather than selling hardware into an open market. This mirrors how utility-scale infrastructure gets built everywhere: anchor demand first, then finance against contracted revenue, then expand the network. Public fast-charging corridors in the region will likely follow the creditworthiness established by these depot contracts, not precede them.

The tradeoff is concentration. An energy-as-a-service model optimizes utilization for the fleets inside the contract, but it can strand capacity that open-access drivers cannot use. The interoperability standards exist — IEC 61851 for the interface, OCPP for the backend, ISO 15118 for the vehicle handshake — yet commercial interoperability, roaming across networks and billing systems, is the layer that remains undecided in most African markets.
