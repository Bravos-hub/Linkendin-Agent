# Option 1 — Mode A (Technical brief)

The architecture of Europe's charging mandate is a capacity-to-fleet ratio, not a charger count. Under AFIR, each member state must sustain at least 1.3 kW of public charging capacity for every battery electric vehicle in its national fleet — a requirement that scales automatically with adoption instead of sitting fixed against it. New analysis from T&E shows the design performing as intended: by the end of March 2026, every EU country except Malta held sufficient public capacity for the BEVs on its roads, and the aggregated national targets overshoot the bloc-level requirement by 180%.

The installed base behind that compliance is substantial. The EU closed 2025 with roughly 1.1 million public charge points — a fivefold increase over the 2020 fleet — and, critically, that capacity was deployed in advance of the strong battery-electric sales growth recorded through 2025 and into 2026. The sequencing matters. Infrastructure was regulated into place ahead of demand rather than trailing it, reversing the chicken-and-egg logic that delayed earlier markets and giving prospective drivers visible assurance before purchase, not after.

AFIR's second mechanism is distance-based: DC fast chargers of 150 kW or more every 60 km along the TEN-T Core and Comprehensive road networks. As of June 2026, 79% of the Core network — primarily national motorways — met the bloc's 2025 ultra-fast target, while 20 of 27 member states have already satisfied the Comprehensive network's 2027 requirement eighteen months ahead of the deadline. The remaining coverage gaps concentrate in Spain and Eastern Europe, which T&E notes are also the fastest-growing ultra-fast networks in the EU.

The binding constraint is now shifting from coverage to usability. T&E calculates that just 70 strategically sited stations — 22 in Spain, 11 in Poland, 7 in Romania — would lift distance-based compliance to 90%. What remains harder to engineer is price transparency, payment interoperability, and dependable access for drivers without home charging, the group most dependent on the public network. Coverage was the engineering problem. Trust is the market-design problem that follows it, and that is where regulation has to move next.
